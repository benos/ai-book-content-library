# Retrospective Narrative Arc — Research Brief

**Purpose:** Guide the sourcing of primary media events for the foundational source entries that will populate the background section of the first book run. The arc was mapped before this brief; this document translates each narrative beat into a sourcing task.

**Sourcing principle:** Link to a primary source (paper, article, talk, book) where one exists. If the ideal source is paywalled or unavailable, a good secondary source that accurately describes the event is acceptable. Do not let source perfection block entry creation.

**Entry types:** Most beats produce one source entry. Character introductions may produce two — one for the event, one for the context note. Let the sourcing process decide; don't pre-split.

---

## Act 1 — The Idea Takes Shape (pre-2012)

### 1. Alan Turing — the founding question

**Narrative purpose:** Establishes the question the entire field is still answering: can machines think? Introduces the intellectual frame that everyone in AI references, consciously or not.

**Looking for:** "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Alan Turing, *Mind*, October 1950. Widely available online. The entry needs the question it poses and why it still structures the debate — not a full summary.

**Source type:** Academic paper
**Entry type:** Event + concept introduction (what the Turing test is, why it matters as a frame)

---

### 2. The AI winters — the pattern of hype and collapse

**Narrative purpose:** Establishes that AI has a history of overpromising and underdelivering. Without this, readers have no frame for reading current claims sceptically. The pattern matters more than any single winter.

**Looking for:** The Lighthill Report (1973) is the clearest primary source for the first winter — a commissioned UK government review that concluded AI had failed to deliver and triggered major funding cuts. For the second winter (late 1980s–early 1990s): coverage of the collapse of expert systems investment. A good retrospective piece covering both winters would also serve.

**Source type:** Government report (Lighthill) + retrospective journalism
**Entry type:** Event (Lighthill as anchor) + interpretive context (the pattern)
**Note:** If Lighthill is hard to obtain cleanly, a well-sourced retrospective article on the AI winters is acceptable.

---

### 3. Ray Kurzweil — the exponential ideology

**Narrative purpose:** Introduces the intellectual operating system of most tech founders and VCs: exponential improvement is inevitable, timelines are predictable, the question is when not if. Understanding this explains why OpenAI, Anthropic, and their investors speak the way they do. The user noted Kurzweil's thesis "has stood the test of time incredibly well" — the entry should reflect that.

**Looking for:** "The Law of Accelerating Returns," Ray Kurzweil, KurzweilAI.net, March 2001 — the essay that formalised the thesis, freely available. *The Singularity Is Near* (2005) is the full development; cite as a book source. A good piece tracking Kurzweil's predictions vs. outcomes (several exist) would strengthen the interpretation.

**Source type:** Essay + book + prediction-tracking journalism
**Entry type:** Character introduction (Kurzweil, the exponential thesis, its influence and track record)

---

### 4. The deep learning lineage — Hinton, LeCun, Bengio

**Narrative purpose:** The academic foundation that preceded the current era. Establishes that the breakthrough wasn't sudden — it was built on decades of unfashionable research by researchers who persisted when the field had moved on. Sets up AlexNet (Act 2) properly.

**Looking for:** A profile piece covering the "three Godfathers" — their decades in the wilderness, why their approach was rejected by mainstream AI, and how vindication came. Hinton's 2006 paper "A fast learning algorithm for deep belief nets" is the key academic source for the neural network revival. The Turing Award announcement (2018) generated good summary coverage.

**Source type:** Profile journalism + academic paper
**Entry type:** Group character introduction

## Act 2 — Proof of Concept (2012–2017)

### 5. AlexNet / ImageNet 2012 — the proof

**Narrative purpose:** The first measurable proof that deep learning worked at scale. The moment tech companies understood they needed to take AI seriously. This is where the current era actually begins.

**Looking for:** "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks," Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton, 2012. Plus tech press coverage of the ImageNet competition results — the margin of victory (~10 percentage points) was what made people take notice.

**Source type:** Academic paper + tech journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry (the paper and result) + context note (what ImageNet was, why beating it mattered)

---

### 6. Google acquires DeepMind (January 2014)

**Narrative purpose:** The moment the talent race became explicit — tech companies realising they needed to own AI research, not just observe it. Also a character introduction for DeepMind and its independence arrangement with Google.

**Looking for:** Press coverage of the acquisition (~£400m/~$650m). The independence agreement (ethics board, no military contracts) is relevant context for later developments.

**Source type:** News journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry + context note on DeepMind (what it is, what it was known for, why Google wanted it)

---

### 7. "Attention Is All You Need" (June 2017)

**Narrative purpose:** The architectural foundation of every major model that followed — GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama all run on the transformer. Not a consumer event, but the most consequential technical paper of the era.

**Looking for:** Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need," Google Brain, 2017. Available on arXiv. The entry needs a plain-language explanation of what the transformer does and why it was better — this is a concept introduction that only needs to happen once.

**Source type:** Academic paper
**Entry type:** Event entry + concept introduction (what a transformer is, at the level of "how it differs from what came before")

---

### 8. OpenAI founding (December 2015)

**Narrative purpose:** Character introduction — who OpenAI is, what it claims to believe, and the contradiction embedded in its structure (non-profit mission, for-profit execution). Understanding this contradiction is essential for reading everything OpenAI does.

**Looking for:** The original OpenAI announcement (December 2015) — available on openai.com. Plus a good retrospective on the founding cast: Altman, Musk, Brockman, Sutskever, Karpathy. The 2019 shift to "capped-profit" structure and the Microsoft investment should be part of the context.

**Source type:** Announcement + retrospective journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry + character introduction (OpenAI as institution, its stated mission and structural contradictions)

## Act 3 — The Scaling Bet (2018–2022)

### 9. GPT-2 "too dangerous to release" (February 2019)

**Narrative purpose:** The first AI safety-as-PR moment. Establishes the pattern of labs framing capability releases as safety decisions. In retrospect, GPT-2 was not dangerous — nothing happened when it was released — which is a useful frame for reading subsequent safety announcements.

**Looking for:** OpenAI's original staged-release announcement (February 2019). Plus the tech press coverage at the time (Wired, MIT Technology Review covered this heavily). Plus the retrospective coverage when OpenAI released the full model six months later and nothing happened.

**Source type:** Announcement + journalism + retrospective
**Entry type:** Event entry (the announcement and its later unravelling as a safety story)

---

### 10. Scaling laws (January 2020)

**Narrative purpose:** The intellectual foundation of the investment frenzy. The insight that model capability scales predictably with compute and data, if you know the formula, means the question becomes who can scale fastest. This paper justified the billions.

**Looking for:** "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," Kaplan et al., OpenAI, January 2020. Available on arXiv. The entry should focus on the implication — predictable scaling = compute investment is rational — rather than the technical detail.

**Source type:** Academic paper
**Entry type:** Event entry + concept introduction (what the scaling hypothesis is and why it matters for understanding investment logic)

---

### 11. GPT-3 (May 2020)

**Narrative purpose:** The first model that surprised researchers. The API. The demos. The beginning of the commercial layer. The moment "AI might be useful" became "AI might be transformative."

**Looking for:** The GPT-3 paper (Brown et al., May 2020). Plus early API access coverage — the demos (poetry, code, translation, reasoning) are the story more than the technical paper.

**Source type:** Academic paper + tech journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry

---

### 12. GitHub Copilot launch (June 2021)

**Narrative purpose:** The first mass-market AI product embedded in a professional workflow, not adjacent to it. The first serious signal that AI assistance would be in the tool, not a separate tool. The model for what came later in design tooling.

**Looking for:** GitHub's Copilot announcement (June 2021) + early reviews and adoption coverage.

**Source type:** Announcement + tech journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry + context note (Copilot as the first example of "AI in the tool you already use")

---

### 13. Stable Diffusion open-source release (August 2022) — LANDMARK

**Narrative purpose:** The image generation moment for designers. DALL-E and Midjourney existed, but Stable Diffusion running locally — free, uncensored, extensible — changed the nature of access. The moment the design profession's relationship with AI became existential rather than theoretical.

**Looking for:** Stability AI's release announcement (August 2022). Plus design and tech press coverage of what open-source image generation meant in practice. The contrast with closed models (DALL-E, Midjourney) is important.

**Source type:** Announcement + design/tech journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry (landmark weight for the designer role)
**Note:** This is the highest-weight single event for designers in the pre-ChatGPT era.

## Act 4 — The Public Moment (late 2022–2023)

### 14. ChatGPT launch (November 2022) — LANDMARK

**Narrative purpose:** The inflection point. Everything before this is context; everything after is the reader's lived experience. 100 million users in two months — the fastest consumer product adoption in history at that point.

**Looking for:** OpenAI's launch announcement (November 30, 2022). Plus mainstream press coverage of the adoption figures. Plus a good piece on what it meant that non-technical people suddenly had direct experience of a frontier AI model.

**Source type:** Announcement + journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry (landmark weight)

---

### 15. Anthropic founding and the safety fork (2021, enters the story here)

**Narrative purpose:** Character introduction — Anthropic, Dario Amodei, the safety-first counter-narrative. The ideological split in the industry crystallises. Understanding what Anthropic is and why it exists changes how you read Claude's releases (including Claude Design).

**Looking for:** Anthropic's founding announcement (April 2021). Profile journalism on Dario Amodei. The "Constitutional AI" paper (December 2022) as the clearest statement of Anthropic's approach. A good piece on why key OpenAI researchers left.

**Source type:** Announcement + profile journalism + academic paper
**Entry type:** Event entry (the split) + character introduction (Anthropic, Amodei, what they represent)

---

### 16. Sam Altman — character introduction and the VC ecosystem

**Narrative purpose:** Who Altman is, what he represents, and why understanding his background changes how you read OpenAI's decisions. This is the entry point for the VC and startup ecosystem as a whole: Y Combinator's philosophy (make something people want, default to launch, growth is the metric), the Silicon Valley value system that underlies most AI development decisions, the financial structure of the industry, and why "move fast" and "AI safety" sit uncomfortably together in the same organisation. Not about Altman personally — he is the character through whom the ecosystem is introduced.

**Looking for:** A good profile of Altman (several exist — The New Yorker, The Atlantic, Wired have all done major pieces). Y Combinator's origin and philosophy (Paul Graham's essays are primary sources for the YC worldview; "Do Things That Don't Scale" and "Default Alive or Default Dead" capture the operating logic). Coverage of the VC funding dynamics in AI — the amounts, the expectations, the concentration.

**Source type:** Profile journalism + founder essays + business journalism
**Entry type:** Character introduction (Altman as entry point; YC and the VC ecosystem as the context note)
**Note:** This entry does not need to be tied to a single event — it's a character introduction triggered by Altman's centrality to the ChatGPT moment. The context note is the VC ecosystem explanation.

---

### 17. GPT-4 (March 2023)

**Narrative purpose:** The bar moves. Multimodal. The model that made the capability question serious for most professions. The first model that designers couldn't dismiss as a novelty.

**Looking for:** OpenAI's announcement (March 2023) + the GPT-4 technical report + mainstream coverage.

**Source type:** Announcement + journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry

---

### 18. EU AI Act — first passage (March 2024)

**Narrative purpose:** The first major regulatory framework for AI. Establishes the EU as the regulatory counterweight to Silicon Valley — with different values and a different theory of risk. Sets the compliance terms that everyone operating in Europe now navigates.

**Looking for:** European Parliament vote coverage (March 2024). The risk-based tiering is the key mechanism to understand (prohibited / high-risk / limited-risk / minimal-risk). Plus a good explanatory piece on what it means in practice.

**Source type:** Policy journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry + character introduction (the EU as actor, its approach to AI governance)

## Act 5 — Infrastructure and Power (2023–2024)

### 19. NVIDIA and the compute story

**Narrative purpose:** Character introduction — Jensen Huang, the GPU supply chain, the physical chokepoint. The insight that AI is a physical infrastructure story as much as a software story. NVIDIA controls a position that no other company controls.

**Looking for:** Profile of Jensen Huang (Fortune, Bloomberg, WSJ have done substantial pieces). Coverage of NVIDIA's market cap surge to $1T+ (mid-2023). The H100 launch and the waiting lists. The broader data centre and power infrastructure story.

**Source type:** Profile journalism + business journalism
**Entry type:** Character introduction (NVIDIA, Huang) + context note (what the compute chokepoint means for everything else)

---

### 20. US export controls on AI chips (October 2022, expanded October 2023)

**Narrative purpose:** The geopolitical dimension of AI. Compute as a national security asset. When AI stopped being a commercial competition and became an explicit matter of state competition. The foundation for understanding the Anthropic geopolitics paper from May 2026.

**Looking for:** Biden administration's October 2022 export control announcement. The October 2023 expansion (broader entity list, new licensing requirements). Coverage of the China implications.

**Source type:** Policy announcement + journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry (two events — link both)

---

### 21. Meta's Llama and the open-source model debate (February 2023, Llama 2 July 2023)

**Narrative purpose:** The open vs. closed debate made concrete. Meta's unusual position — frontier-lab capability, open release — created the ecosystem of tools and fine-tunes that closed labs could not. Sets up the broader argument about what "open" means for AI safety and access.

**Looking for:** Meta's Llama announcement (February 2023). The safety controversy when Llama 1 leaked. The Llama 2 commercial release (July 2023). Mark Zuckerberg's public arguments for openness.

**Source type:** Announcement + journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry

## Act 6 — The Application Layer (2024–2025)

### 22. The agentic turn — Claude computer use (October 2024)

**Narrative purpose:** Models that act, not just answer. The shift from tool to agent changes what AI is and changes the design problem — you are no longer designing for a human performing discrete actions. Anthropic's computer use demo was the clearest public demonstration.

**Looking for:** Anthropic's computer use announcement (October 2024). Coverage of what it demonstrated and what it implied.

**Source type:** Announcement + journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry + concept introduction (what "agentic" means and why it changes the design problem)

---

### 23. The AI design tool category — v0, Lovable, Bolt (2023–2024)

**Narrative purpose:** The application layer specific to designers. Specialist tools proving the market before frontier labs entered. Context for understanding Claude Design and Figma Make as the category's second wave.

**Looking for:** v0 launch coverage (Vercel, late 2023). Lovable and Bolt coverage. A piece assessing the category as it existed before frontier labs entered.

**Source type:** Announcement + tech journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry (probably one entry covering the category, not three separate entries)

---

### 24. The employment picture — first serious data (2023–2024)

**Narrative purpose:** What's actually being automated vs. augmented in practice. The reader needs real data alongside the competing narratives (AI will take all jobs / AI will create more jobs than it destroys). This entry should present the evidence honestly — including what it doesn't yet show.

**Looking for:** Goldman Sachs "The Potentially Large Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Economic Growth" (March 2023) — widely cited, quantifies automation exposure by job category. IMF analysis on AI and labour markets (January 2024). Academic papers on task-level displacement (Erik Brynjolfsson's work at Stanford). The entry should include sources that complicate the benign narrative alongside those that support it.

**Source type:** Research reports + academic papers + journalism
**Entry type:** Event entry (use the Goldman report as anchor; synthesise across sources)
