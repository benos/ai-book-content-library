# Source Entries — Act 1: The Idea Takes Shape (pre-2012)

Retrospective foundational entries. Four beats covering the intellectual and academic foundations of the current AI era.

---

### Can machines think? — Turing poses the founding question

**Date:** October 1950

**Facts:** Alan Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," published in *Mind* (Vol. 59, No. 236), opens with the question: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" Turing immediately notes the question cannot be answered as stated — "machine" and "think" resist definition — and replaces it with a behavioural test: can a machine perform indistinguishably from a human in a text-based exchange? He calls this the Imitation Game. The paper addresses nine objections to machine intelligence (theological, mathematical via Gödel, consciousness, Lady Lovelace's originality objection, and others) and predicts: "By the end of the century it will indeed be technologically possible to program a machine to play the game." He also proposes the "child machine" approach: program a machine at child-like capability and subject it to education via reward and punishment.

**Interpretation:** The lasting significance is the reframe. By substituting a behavioural question for a philosophical one, Turing made machine intelligence empirically investigable rather than definitionally impossible. The nine objections he addresses are essentially the same objections raised in every AI debate for the 75 years that followed — about consciousness, creativity, originality, and moral standing. His end-of-century prediction proved broadly accurate for narrow language tasks. The specific term "Turing test" is a later coinage; Turing himself called it the Imitation Game, and the popular framing strips out much of his nuance.

**Open questions:** Does passing the Turing test demonstrate intelligence or sophisticated imitation? Searle's Chinese Room argument (1980) is the most developed form of this objection and remains unresolved. Whether LLMs that pass informal versions of the Turing test are "thinking" in any meaningful sense is still genuinely contested.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** This paper set the intellectual frame within which AI capability has been debated ever since; every conversation about whether AI "really understands" is a descendant of this question.

**Context notes:** Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician who made foundational contributions to computation theory and to breaking the Enigma cipher during World War II. He was prosecuted by the British government for homosexuality in 1952 and died in 1954, aged 41. The intellectual frame he established — can machines behave indistinguishably from thinkers, and what would that mean? — is still the structure within which AI capability debates occur. The term "Turing test" was not Turing's own; it was popularised later by others summarising his work.

**Sources:** Alan M. Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," *Mind*, Vol. 59, No. 236, pp. 433–460, October 1950 — https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/471/papers/turing.pdf

### The AI winters — overpromise, underfunding, collapse (1966–1993)

**Date:** 1973 (Lighthill Report as primary anchor); pattern runs 1966–1993

**Facts:** AI research suffered two major funding collapses. The first winter was triggered by a series of failures: the 1966 ALPAC report ended US machine translation funding ($20m spent, no usable output); Minsky and Papert's 1969 *Perceptrons* demonstrated fundamental limits of single-layer networks; the Mansfield Amendment (1969) required DARPA to fund only mission-oriented research, forcing researchers to promise immediate practical results they could not deliver. The Lighthill Report (1973) — commissioned by the UK Science Research Council, written by Sir James Lighthill, Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge — delivered the verdict: "In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised." Its primary technical criticism was combinatorial explosion: AI techniques worked in restricted domains but failed to scale. The UK ended AI support at most universities; the effects spread internationally. The second winter (1987–1993) was triggered by the collapse of the LISP machine market — general-purpose computers became cheaper and more powerful, eliminating a half-billion-dollar specialised hardware industry in roughly a year — and the failure of expert systems, which proved brittle, expensive to maintain, and unable to learn. Japan's Fifth Generation computing project, which had generated enormous hype since 1981, failed to deliver. Over 300 AI companies shut down or were acquired by 1993.

**Interpretation:** The winters established a pattern that shaped how serious people read AI claims for decades: optimistic predictions secure funding, real-world complexity defeats the approaches, credibility collapses. This is why technologists with institutional memory were still sceptical of ChatGPT in late 2022 — they had seen this before. The winters also explain why the current era is structured around commercial deployment rather than research promises: the lesson the field absorbed was that research capability must produce economic value or funding disappears. Whether the current era has broken the pattern or is simply running a longer cycle is genuinely contested.

**Open questions:** The winters occurred before mass commercial adoption; the current era has achieved deployment at a scale no previous wave did. Does this make the current moment structurally different, or just further along the same curve?

**Historical weight:** significant
**Historical weight note:** The winters are essential context for reading AI claims with appropriate scepticism; they establish the recurring pattern of overpromise and correction that preceded the current era.

**Context notes:** The Lighthill Report is notable because its author was a distinguished mathematician commissioned by government — not an industry critic with an agenda. Its verdict carried unusual institutional weight. The report's core critique — that AI worked in "toy" environments but could not scale — is essentially the critique levelled at every AI wave until the current one.

**Sources:** James Lighthill, "Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey," Science Research Council (UK), 1973 — summary at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighthill_report; AI winter history at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_winter

### Ray Kurzweil proposes the Law of Accelerating Returns

**Date:** March 7, 2001 (essay); expanded in *The Singularity Is Near* (2005)

**Facts:** Kurzweil's essay "The Law of Accelerating Returns," published on KurzweilAI.net, argues that technological change is exponential rather than linear, and that the rate of acceleration itself accelerates. Core claim: "We won't experience 100 years of progress in the 21st century — it will be more like 20,000 years of progress (at today's rate)." Specific predictions: human-level AI by approximately 2029; the Singularity (AI surpassing all human intelligence) by approximately 2045. Kurzweil was hired as a Director of Engineering at Google in 2012. Prediction track record: Kurzweil claims 86% accuracy across ~147 quantifiable predictions from his 1990 book; an independent 2019 assessment (Stuart Armstrong, Future of Humanity Institute) put the figure at approximately 42%. Verified correct predictions include chess-playing computers defeating world champions (predicted; occurred 1997 with Deep Blue), commercial viability of speech recognition, and genome sequencing costs falling below $1,000 by 2020 (hit $1,000 in 2017).

**Interpretation:** The Law of Accelerating Returns became the intellectual operating system for Silicon Valley's approach to AI timelines. Its significance is less about predictive accuracy than ideological influence: the argument that exponential curves make current limitations irrelevant to near-future possibilities gave investors and founders a framework for betting on AI before it was commercially viable. When tech founders and VCs describe AI as "inevitable" and dismiss near-term limitations as temporary, they are largely reasoning within this framework, often without knowing its source. The broad direction — exponential improvement in computing capability, commercially viable AI as a near-term reality — has been more vindicated than the specific dates.

**Open questions:** Whether the current AI moment validates the full Singularity thesis (intelligence recursively improving itself) or merely validates the exponential hardware and data thesis is genuinely open. The 2029 human-level AI prediction is approaching its test date.

**Historical weight:** significant
**Historical weight note:** The essay and book directly shaped how tech founders and investors reason about AI timelines and urgency; understanding Kurzweil's framework is necessary context for interpreting industry behaviour.

**Context notes:** Ray Kurzweil (born 1948) is an inventor and futurist whose practical work spans OCR, text-to-speech, speech recognition software, and music synthesis. The essay and book are important not as a prediction ledger to verify but as the intellectual framework that has shaped how the people building and funding AI reason about what they are doing and why now. The question "is Kurzweil right?" is less useful than "why do so many serious people think he is?"

**Sources:** Ray Kurzweil, "The Law of Accelerating Returns," KurzweilAI.net, March 7, 2001 — archived at https://writingsbyraykurzweil.com; Ray Kurzweil, *The Singularity Is Near*, Viking, 2005

### The deep learning lineage — Hinton, LeCun, Bengio and the wilderness years

**Date:** July 2006 (Hinton's "Fast Learning Algorithm" paper as the reignition event; the underlying period is 1980s–2012)

**Facts:** Geoffrey Hinton (British-Canadian, born 1947) championed neural network approaches through the 1980s and 1990s when mainstream AI had moved to symbolic reasoning and later to support vector machines. Key early work: backpropagation (1986, with Rumelhart and Williams), Boltzmann machines (1985). In 2006 he published "A Fast Learning Algorithm for Deep Belief Nets" (with Osindero and Teh), demonstrating a method for training deep networks by stacking restricted Boltzmann machines layer by layer — overcoming the vanishing gradient problem that had made deep networks practically untrainable. Yann LeCun (French, born 1960) developed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in the late 1980s, demonstrating LeNet-5 on handwritten digit recognition; CNNs became the foundational architecture for computer vision. Yoshua Bengio (French-Canadian, born 1965) published a 2003 paper on neural probabilistic language models introducing high-dimensional word embeddings — a direct precursor to modern LLMs. Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, all three continued this work when "few read" their papers and mainstream ML had largely written off deep networks as theoretically problematic. The 2018 ACM Turing Award (shared equally, $1m prize) cited them "for conceptual and engineering breakthroughs that have made deep neural networks a critical component of computing — for over 30 years, these visionaries persisted with their belief that training deep neural networks could yield impressive results." Hinton received the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics.

**Interpretation:** The wilderness years matter because they establish that the current era was a vindication of a decades-long research bet, not a sudden invention. The three worked in relative isolation — Bengio in Montreal, LeCun at Bell Labs then NYU, Hinton moving between the UK, US, and Canada partly due to funding difficulties — on methods the broader field considered obsolete. The 2006 paper was the internal turning point; AlexNet in 2012 (built by Hinton's students Krizhevsky and Sutskever) was the public one — covered in the Act 2 entries. Understanding this lineage explains why the field refers to "godfathers": there are specific people whose three-decade persistence created the conditions for everything that followed.

**Open questions:** Whether current large language models represent the kind of intelligence Hinton and colleagues envisioned or a fundamentally different phenomenon is actively debated. Hinton departed Google in 2023 specifically to speak freely about AI risks he had not anticipated when building the foundations — the gap between his optimism as a researcher and his concern as an observer is itself a significant data point.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The work of these three researchers is the direct technical foundation of the entire current AI era; without the neural network revival they sustained through the wilderness years, the transformer and everything built on it would not exist.

**Context notes:** Hinton, LeCun, and Bengio are collectively referred to as the "Godfathers of AI" or "Godfathers of deep learning." Their significance is not as current lab builders but as the researchers who proved neural networks worked, against sustained mainstream scepticism, across three decades. When you encounter the terms neural network, convolutional network, transformer, or large language model, you are reading about technology that descends directly from their work. Hinton's 2023 departure from Google to warn about AI risk adds a dimension worth registering: the person most responsible for enabling the current AI era is now among its most prominent cautioners.

**Sources:** G. E. Hinton, S. Osindero, Y.-W. Teh, "A Fast Learning Algorithm for Deep Belief Nets," *Neural Computation*, Vol. 18, No. 7, July 2006 — https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/fastnc.pdf; ACM Turing Award 2018 — https://awards.acm.org/about/2018-turing
