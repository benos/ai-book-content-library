# Source Entries — Act 2: Proof of Concept (2012–2017)

Retrospective foundational entries. Four beats covering the period from deep learning's first public proof to the architectural foundation of the current era.

---

### AlexNet Wins the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge

**Date:** September 30, 2012 (competition submission); results announced October 2012; paper published at NeurIPS, December 2012

**Facts:** On September 30, 2012, a team called SuperVision — Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and their PhD supervisor Geoffrey Hinton, all at the University of Toronto — submitted a deep convolutional neural network to the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC). Their network, later called AlexNet, achieved a top-5 error rate of 15.3%, against 26.2% for the second-place entry: a margin of 10.8 percentage points. No prior year had seen an improvement of more than a few points. The network contained 60 million parameters across eight layers (five convolutional, three fully connected) and was trained on two NVIDIA GTX 580 GPUs with 3 GB of memory each — the dual-GPU split was required because no single GPU could hold the full model. The paper, "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks," was published at NeurIPS in December 2012. ImageNet itself was a dataset of over 14 million labelled images across 22,000 categories, assembled from 2007 onward by Fei-Fei Li and collaborators at Stanford, using Amazon Mechanical Turk for labelling at scale. The ILSVRC used a subset of 1.2 million images across 1,000 categories. The "top-5 error rate" metric means: given an image, the model proposes five guesses; it is scored as wrong only if the correct label is not among those five.

**Interpretation:** AlexNet did not introduce any entirely new component in isolation. Deep networks, convolutional layers, and GPU training each had precursors. What it did was demonstrate — with a number — that the combination worked at a scale and margin that could not be explained away. The 10.8-point gap was not a marginal improvement over a known technique; it was a discontinuous jump that invalidated the prior consensus that hand-crafted features were necessary for visual recognition. Yann LeCun called it "an unequivocal turning point in the history of computer vision." Within two years the field had essentially abandoned the previous approach. The effect radiated outward: tech companies that had treated AI as a background research concern began acquiring AI talent aggressively. The acquisition of DeepMind by Google eighteen months later is a direct downstream consequence of what ImageNet 2012 signalled.

**Open questions:** How much of the breakthrough was architecture versus data scale versus GPU availability — and whether AlexNet would have won had the same ideas been tried two years earlier with less data — remains analytically contested. The degree to which Hinton's prior neural network research was essential versus the engineering contributions of Krizhevsky is often simplified in popular retelling.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** AlexNet is the clearest single moment where the modern AI era can be said to begin — it converted deep learning from a minority position in computer vision into the dominant paradigm, and triggered the talent and acquisition race that defines the industry since.

**Context notes:** ImageNet is a large labelled image dataset, first assembled by computer scientist Fei-Fei Li starting in 2007, that became the shared testing ground for computer vision. The annual ILSVRC competition used a standardised subset to make results comparable across teams. "Top-5 error rate" is the fraction of test images for which the correct label falls outside the model's top five guesses — a lower number is better. A convolutional neural network (CNN) is a type of neural network designed to detect visual patterns hierarchically; "deep" refers to having many layers stacked in sequence.

**Sources:** "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks," Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton, NeurIPS 2012 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/2999134.2999257; "AlexNet," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlexNet

### Google Acquires DeepMind

**Date:** January 26–27, 2014

**Facts:** On January 26–27, 2014, Google confirmed it had acquired DeepMind Technologies, a London-based AI research laboratory, for a price reported as approximately £400 million (~$500–650 million USD). DeepMind had been founded in November 2010 by Demis Hassabis, Shane Legg, and Mustafa Suleyman. Hassabis and Legg had met at the Gatsby Computational Neuroscience Unit at University College London. By late 2013, DeepMind had demonstrated a deep reinforcement learning system — the Deep Q-Network (DQN) — that learned to play 49 Atari games from raw pixel input at superhuman level, a result that circulated privately before publication. Facebook was also in acquisition talks with DeepMind at the time; Google outbid them. As a condition of the acquisition, DeepMind's founders extracted two commitments from Google: the creation of an ethics board to oversee use of DeepMind's AI technology, and a prohibition on the technology being used for military or surveillance purposes. The ethics board's membership was never made public. AlphaGo, the system that would defeat world Go champion Lee Sedol, was still two years away at the time of acquisition.

**Interpretation:** The DeepMind acquisition was the first large-scale demonstration that AI research capability was being treated as a strategic asset worth acquiring at platform-company prices. The bidding war with Facebook underlined that this was not idiosyncratic to Google; the two largest advertising platforms simultaneously concluded that AI research talent was sufficiently scarce and sufficiently important to pay nine-figure sums for laboratories that had produced no revenue. The independence conditions DeepMind extracted — ethics board, no military use — were significant not because they held (both weakened substantially over the following decade) but because they reveal DeepMind's founders understood they were entering a context where those protections would be needed. The acquisition established the template for how frontier AI research would be funded: not through grants or public institutions, but through acquisition by or investment from technology companies with existing infrastructure and capital.

**Open questions:** The ethics board's composition was never disclosed and its actual function, if any, remains unclear. The no-military-use condition has effectively lapsed — Google DeepMind technology has been sold to governments including militaries — but the legal status of the original commitment and whether it was ever formally rescinded has not been publicly established.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The DeepMind acquisition was the moment when acquiring AI research capability became an explicit competitive strategy among major technology companies, establishing the structural pattern — private capital funding frontier AI research — that has defined the field since.

**Context notes:** DeepMind was a London AI research laboratory with a dual identity: academically rigorous (publishing in peer-reviewed journals, pursuing fundamental research) and explicitly aimed at artificial general intelligence. Its founders came from neuroscience, cognitive science, and computer science backgrounds. Demis Hassabis had previously founded a video game company (Elixir Studios) and holds a PhD in cognitive neuroscience from UCL. DeepMind's early research used games as controlled environments to test general learning algorithms — a methodology that produced AlphaGo, AlphaFold, and a series of landmark results across the following decade.

**Sources:** "Google Acquires Artificial Intelligence Startup DeepMind For More Than $500M," TechCrunch, January 26, 2014 — https://techcrunch.com/2014/01/26/google-deepmind/; "Google DeepMind," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_DeepMind

### OpenAI Founded as a Non-Profit AI Research Laboratory

**Date:** December 11, 2015

**Facts:** OpenAI was publicly announced on December 11, 2015, as a non-profit artificial intelligence research organisation. Its founding charter stated: "Our goal is to advance digital intelligence in the way that is most likely to benefit humanity as a whole, unconstrained by a need to generate financial return." Eleven co-founders were listed: Sam Altman (then president of Y Combinator), Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever, Elon Musk, Wojciech Zaremba, Andrej Karpathy, John Schulman, Trevor Blackwell, Vicki Cheung, Durk Kingma, and Pamela Vagata. Funders pledged a combined $1 billion, though this was a pledge rather than a cash deposit. In 2019, OpenAI created a for-profit subsidiary, OpenAI LP, structured as a "capped-profit" entity in which investor returns were capped at 100 times their investment; Microsoft subsequently invested $1 billion. Musk had left the board in February 2018, officially citing a potential conflict of interest with Tesla's AI work; court testimony in 2026 revealed a failed attempt to take personal control of the organisation was a significant underlying factor. By 2025, OpenAI had completed a further restructuring converting the for-profit subsidiary into a public benefit corporation with the original non-profit retaining a 26% ownership stake.

**Interpretation:** OpenAI was founded on a legible contradiction. Its stated mission — ensuring AGI "benefits all of humanity" — required it to build AGI before less safety-conscious actors did; but building AGI required capital at a scale a non-profit could not sustain; and attracting that capital required offering returns, which the non-profit structure formally prohibited. The 2019 capped-profit structure attempted to square this circle by making the profit motive explicit while nominally constraining it. The contradiction was not accidental or concealed — it was inherent to the founding premise and has remained the central tension in every subsequent public debate about OpenAI's direction, governance, and credibility. OpenAI's founding also established the genre of the "safety-motivated" AI lab: an organisation that justifies competitive AI development on the grounds that it is better for safety-conscious people to be at the frontier than to cede that ground to others. This logic — build fast to be safe — became the template argument for the field.

**Open questions:** Whether the non-profit governance structure ever meaningfully constrained decisions, or served primarily as a reputational signal, is contested and was the central question in Musk's litigation against OpenAI as of 2026. Whether "capped-profit" structures offer genuine governance constraints or are formalistic is an open question in corporate law.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** OpenAI introduced the structural and rhetorical template — safety mission combined with private capital and competitive development — that defined institutional AI from 2015 onward, and its founding cast became the central characters of the subsequent decade.

**Context notes:** OpenAI is an AI research and deployment organisation founded in San Francisco. It began as a non-profit, restructured into a hybrid capped-profit model in 2019, and underwent further restructuring toward a public benefit corporation in 2025. Its relationship with Microsoft — which made initial and subsequent multi-billion-dollar investments in exchange for cloud infrastructure exclusivity and a share of revenues — became the dominant fact of its commercial existence. Ilya Sutskever, one of the co-founders, had been a key contributor to AlexNet; his presence at OpenAI was a direct line between the 2012 ImageNet breakthrough and the 2015 founding. Sam Altman became CEO and the company's primary public face.

**Sources:** "Introducing OpenAI," OpenAI, December 11, 2015 — https://openai.com/index/introducing-openai/; "OpenAI Charter," OpenAI — https://openai.com/charter/; "OpenAI," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI

### "Attention Is All You Need" — The Transformer Architecture

**Date:** June 12, 2017 (arXiv preprint); published at NeurIPS, December 2017

**Facts:** On June 12, 2017, eight researchers at Google — Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser, and Illia Polosukhin — posted a preprint titled "Attention Is All You Need" to arXiv (1706.03762). The paper proposed a new neural network architecture called the Transformer, which dispensed entirely with recurrent layers and convolutional layers, relying solely on a mechanism called self-attention. The problem it addressed was a fundamental bottleneck in the then-dominant recurrent neural network (RNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM) architectures: because RNNs process sequences one token at a time, they cannot be parallelised across a sequence during training, making them slow and difficult to scale. The Transformer replaced this sequential processing with self-attention, computing relationships between all positions in a sequence simultaneously. On the standard machine translation benchmarks of the time, the Transformer achieved state-of-the-art results on English-to-German and English-to-French translation after as little as twelve hours of training on eight P100 GPUs. As of 2025 the paper had been cited more than 173,000 times, placing it among the ten most-cited papers of the 21st century. GPT-1, BERT, GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Llama, and virtually every large language model since are built on the transformer architecture.

**Interpretation:** "Attention Is All You Need" is the most consequential technical paper of the 2010s AI wave. The transformer did not merely improve on RNNs; it removed the architectural constraint — sequential computation — that had placed a ceiling on how large and capable language models could become. By making training parallelisable, it made it possible to scale with hardware at a rate recurrent networks could not absorb. The direct path from this paper to ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini is unambiguous: all are transformers. The paper is unusual in that its full consequences were not visible for several years — its impact on language was not immediately clear from a machine translation result — but from 2020 onward it became apparent that the transformer had quietly provided the substrate for the entire subsequent era.

**Open questions:** Whether the transformer architecture will remain dominant or be succeeded by alternatives (state space models and similar) is genuinely open; none had displaced it at scale as of mid-2026. Credit allocation among the eight co-authors is contested; Jakob Uszkoreit has noted he proposed the core idea of replacing recurrence with self-attention.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The transformer is the architectural foundation on which every significant large language model since 2018 has been built; this paper made the current AI moment technically possible.

**Context notes:** A transformer is a type of neural network that processes text by computing, for each word or token in a sequence, a weighted relationship to every other word in the sequence simultaneously — rather than reading the sequence step by step. This mechanism, called self-attention, allows the model to capture long-range dependencies without information degrading over distance. Prior to the transformer, the dominant architectures for language tasks were recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), both of which processed sequences serially and could not be efficiently parallelised on modern GPU hardware. The transformer's parallelisability was what made training at the scale of billions of parameters practical.

**Sources:** "Attention Is All You Need," Vaswani et al., arXiv:1706.03762, June 12, 2017 — https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762; "Attention Is All You Need," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention_Is_All_You_Need
