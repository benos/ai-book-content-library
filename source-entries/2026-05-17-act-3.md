# Source Entries — Act 3: The Scaling Bet (2018–2022)

Retrospective foundational entries. Five beats covering GPT-2 through Stable Diffusion.

---

### GPT-2: "Too Dangerous to Release"

**Date:** February 14, 2019 (partial announcement); November 5, 2019 (full model released)

**Facts:** On February 14, 2019, OpenAI published a blog post announcing GPT-2, a 1.5-billion-parameter language model trained on WebText — approximately 40 GB of text derived from 45 million outbound links posted to Reddit with at least 3 karma. OpenAI declined to release the full model weights, citing concerns about malicious use: fake news generation, impersonation, spam automation, and phishing. Instead, they released a smaller 117-million-parameter version and described a "staged release" strategy. The 345M, 762M, and finally 1.5B models were released incrementally through the year. The full model was released on November 5, 2019. OpenAI's own post-release assessment stated: "We've seen no strong evidence of misuse so far." No verified incident of harmful deployment was documented.

**Interpretation:** GPT-2 was not, in retrospect, especially dangerous — it was an impressive text predictor, not a disinformation weapon. What OpenAI actually invented here was a public-relations template: frame a capability release as a safety decision, generate coverage that simultaneously establishes the lab as a responsible actor and as the owner of the most powerful model in existence. The pattern would replay at every major release thereafter. Critics at the time noted that the model was easily replicable by well-resourced adversaries regardless of whether OpenAI published it, making the "staged" logic more theatrical than protective. The episode is historically significant not for the technology but for the institutional behaviour it inaugurated: safety discourse as competitive positioning.

**Open questions:** Whether OpenAI genuinely believed GPT-2 was dangerous, or whether the framing was a calculated communications decision, has never been settled. The question of what legitimate staged release looks like — as opposed to capability hype dressed as caution — remains actively debated with each new frontier model.

**Historical weight:** significant
**Historical weight note:** GPT-2 established the rhetorical playbook for AI capability announcements — "responsible release" as a genre — but the model itself was not a technical inflection point.

**Sources:** "Better Language Models and Their Implications," OpenAI, February 14, 2019 — https://openai.com/index/better-language-models/; "GPT-2," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-2

### Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

**Date:** January 23, 2020

**Facts:** Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, Tom Henighan, Tom B. Brown, Benjamin Chess, Rewon Child, Scott Gray, Alec Radford, Jeffrey Wu, and Dario Amodei — all then at OpenAI — published "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (arXiv:2001.08361). The paper trained models ranging from 768,000 to 1.5 billion parameters on datasets ranging from 22 million to 23 billion tokens, systematically varying model size, dataset size, and compute budget. The central finding: test loss decreases as a power law with each of the three variables — model size (N), dataset size (D), and compute (C) — with the relationships holding across more than seven orders of magnitude. Crucially, the paper showed that for a fixed compute budget, it is better to train a larger model on fewer tokens than a smaller model to convergence, and that larger models are significantly more sample-efficient.

**Interpretation:** This paper gave the scaling hypothesis a quantitative backbone. Before it, the intuition that "bigger is better" was empirical folklore; after it, the relationship was a predictable curve with specific exponents. The practical implication was stark: if you could raise more capital and acquire more compute than anyone else, you would, almost mechanically, produce a more capable model. This reframed AI research as an infrastructure competition and directly justified the tens of billions of dollars that would pour into GPU clusters over the following five years. The Dario Amodei co-authorship is notable: he would leave OpenAI to found Anthropic in 2021, taking the scaling-law worldview with him as a founding thesis.

**Open questions:** The Kaplan scaling laws were partly superseded by the Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al., DeepMind, 2022), which corrected the compute-optimal training prescription — Kaplan et al. underweighted the dataset-size term. The broader question of whether power-law scaling continues indefinitely, or whether capability walls exist at larger scales, remains empirically open.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** By converting the intuition that scale drives capability into a precise mathematical relationship, this paper provided the intellectual justification for the investment frenzy that followed and set the terms of competition for the rest of the decade.

**Sources:** "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," Kaplan et al., OpenAI, arXiv:2001.08361, January 23, 2020 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361

### GPT-3 and the Arrival of Few-Shot Learning

**Date:** May 28, 2020

**Facts:** Tom B. Brown, Benjamin Mann, Nick Ryder, and 28 additional OpenAI researchers published "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners," introducing GPT-3 — at the time the largest language model ever trained, with 175 billion parameters. The model was trained on a filtered version of Common Crawl, WebText2, Books1, Books2, and English Wikipedia — roughly 300 billion tokens total. The key finding was an emergent behaviour: GPT-3 could perform tasks it had not been explicitly fine-tuned for by conditioning on a small number of examples in the prompt (few-shot), a single example (one-shot), or just a task description (zero-shot), without any weight updates. Access was provided through a private API with a waitlist from June 2020; OpenAI did not release model weights. The API became generally available in November 2021. Early beta user Gwern Branwen published extensive experiments documenting qualitative capabilities — code generation, legal text, analogy reasoning, essay completion — that surprised observers including himself.

**Interpretation:** GPT-3 was the first model that crossed a qualitative threshold for a broad audience outside ML research. Previous models required fine-tuning to change their behaviour; GPT-3 could be redirected in real time by any literate person with an API key. This made it, for the first time, a tool rather than a research artefact. The private API — not open weights — was a deliberate commercial architecture: capability without reproducibility, access without commoditisation. It established OpenAI's business model for everything that followed. The model's surprises also deepened the scaling-law programme and raised the first serious public discussions about whether extremely large models might be unsafe because of emergent properties that designers could not predict.

**Open questions:** The mechanism behind in-context learning — whether the model is retrieving patterns from training data, performing something like rapid approximate inference, or doing something else — remains theoretically contested. Whether the API-only release model meaningfully impeded capability diffusion, given that similar models emerged elsewhere within 18 months, is unresolved.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** GPT-3 was the first model that made the general public and non-ML professionals viscerally encounter what a large language model could do, and its API-only release model defined the commercial architecture every subsequent major lab copied or consciously rejected.

**Context notes:** GPT-3 is accessed through an API — a technical gateway that allows external developers and applications to query the model over the internet, without accessing the model itself. OpenAI's decision to release via API rather than publishing model weights meant that the capability existed only as a service OpenAI controlled and could charge for — a significant departure from the academic norm of publishing model weights alongside papers.

**Sources:** "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners," Brown et al., OpenAI, arXiv:2005.14165, May 28, 2020 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165; "The Scaling Hypothesis," Gwern Branwen — https://gwern.net/scaling-hypothesis

### GitHub Copilot: AI Inside the Professional Tool

**Date:** June 29, 2021 (technical preview); June 21, 2022 (general availability)

**Facts:** On June 29, 2021, GitHub announced GitHub Copilot in technical preview — an AI code-completion tool embedded directly in Visual Studio Code. Copilot was built on OpenAI Codex, a model derived from GPT-3 and fine-tuned on approximately 159 GB of Python code from 54 million public GitHub repositories. It suggested whole lines and complete functions in real time, inline with the developer's cursor. General availability launched June 21, 2022, at $10/month for individuals. By 2022, Copilot was contributing approximately 27% of code written by its users. On November 3, 2022, the Joseph Saveri Law Firm filed a class-action lawsuit against GitHub, Microsoft, and OpenAI, alleging Copilot violated open-source licences by training on code without attribution or consent and reproducing verbatim code snippets — GitHub acknowledged roughly 1% of suggestions may contain code longer than 150 characters matching the training set.

**Interpretation:** Copilot was the proof of concept that AI could be embedded in a professional workflow rather than accessed as a separate tool. The distinction matters: a standalone AI assistant requires a deliberate act of switching context; an assistant that lives inside the environment where work happens can become invisible infrastructure. This is the model that spread to design tools, writing tools, spreadsheet software, and presentation software over the following three years. Copilot also established the copyright problem that would animate every subsequent AI content litigation — whether training on publicly available work constitutes fair use, and whether outputs that reproduce that work are infringing.

**Open questions:** The November 2022 lawsuit was the first major legal test of AI training and output copyright and was still working through courts years later. Whether embedding AI in professional tools accelerates skill atrophy — by reducing deliberate practice — is a genuine and largely uninvestigated question.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** Copilot was the first AI product to demonstrate that embedding a model inside an existing professional tool — rather than creating a new destination — was the dominant product architecture, and its copyright controversy established the legal fault line every AI content product would face thereafter.

**Context notes:** GitHub is the world's largest platform for storing and collaborating on software code, hosting over 100 million repositories and used by the substantial majority of professional software developers. Microsoft acquired GitHub in 2018 for $7.5 billion. The significance of embedding AI inside GitHub specifically was that it placed the model at the exact moment of production — where a developer is writing code — rather than as an adjacent resource they would consult separately.

**Sources:** "Introducing GitHub Copilot: Your AI Pair Programmer," GitHub Blog, June 29, 2021 — https://github.blog/news-insights/product-news/introducing-github-copilot-ai-pair-programmer/; "GitHub Copilot," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GitHub_Copilot

### Stable Diffusion: Open-Source Image Generation

**Date:** August 22, 2022

**Facts:** On August 22, 2022, Stability AI publicly released Stable Diffusion 1.4 — model weights, code, and all — under a CreativeML Open RAIL-M licence. The model was developed collaboratively by researchers from Stability AI, the CompVis Group at Ludwig Maximilian University Munich, and RunwayML, trained on a 2-billion-image subset of LAION-5B — a dataset of 5 billion text-image pairs assembled from web crawls. Stable Diffusion used a latent diffusion architecture, compressing images into a lower-dimensional space before applying the diffusion process, making it runnable on consumer hardware: within days of release, the community had it operating on Windows laptops, M1 Macs, and home servers with a 4GB model footprint. By contrast, OpenAI's DALL-E 2 (released April 2022) was available only via a closed waitlist API; Midjourney (launched July 2022) was a closed SaaS product accessible only through Discord. Stability AI's CEO Emad Mostaque led the release. The LAION-5B dataset was subsequently found to contain 3,226 suspected links to child sexual abuse material; LAION temporarily took down the dataset in late 2023. A class-action lawsuit from artists against Stability AI, Midjourney, and DeviantArt was filed in January 2023.

**Interpretation:** Stable Diffusion did not merely release an image-generation model; it changed the political economy of image generation. DALL-E and Midjourney were products — access controlled, content moderated, commercially managed. Stable Diffusion was infrastructure. Any person with a mid-range GPU could run it without an account, without content restrictions, without usage limits. For the creative community, the capability was not coming as a future subscription service: it was already here, running locally. The design profession's encounter with AI image generation had been mediated by waitlists and paywalls; Stable Diffusion removed both. The training data controversy — hundreds of millions of artists' works ingested without notice, consent, or compensation — became structurally inescapable because the model weights were public, making the training decision permanent and visible. This is why Stable Diffusion, rather than DALL-E or Midjourney, marks the moment the design profession's relationship with AI became existential rather than curious.

**Open questions:** Whether training on web-scraped images without artist consent constitutes copyright infringement remains undecided in US courts. The question of whether open-sourcing powerful generative models causes net harm (by removing guardrails) or net benefit (by preventing monopolisation) is one of the defining unresolved debates in AI policy.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** Stable Diffusion's open-source release made text-to-image generation genuinely accessible to anyone with a laptop, transforming professional image generation from a gated product into public infrastructure and making the questions of artist consent and creative labour displacement suddenly concrete rather than theoretical.

**Context notes:** Stability AI was a London-based AI startup founded by Emad Mostaque, a former hedge fund manager. "Open source" in this context means the model weights — the trained numerical parameters that encode the model's behaviour — were published for free download alongside the code, so anyone could run the model locally, modify it, fine-tune it on different datasets, and build other software on top of it. This is qualitatively different from DALL-E (which required an OpenAI API key, routed requests through OpenAI's servers, and applied content filtering) and Midjourney (a closed commercial SaaS product). Running locally meant no usage logs, no content policy enforcement, no per-image cost, and no dependency on an internet connection or a company's continued operation.

**Sources:** "Open Source AI Image Generator Stable Diffusion Released to the Public," PetaPixel, August 23, 2022 — https://petapixel.com/2022/08/23/open-source-ai-image-generator-stable-diffusion-released-to-the-public/; "Stable Diffusion," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_Diffusion
