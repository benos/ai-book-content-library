# Source Entries — Act 4: The Public Moment (Late 2022–2023)

Retrospective foundational entries. Five beats covering ChatGPT through the EU AI Act.

Note: The EU AI Act was passed in March 2024, making it technically Act 5 chronologically, but it is included here as the regulatory response to the Act 4 public moment — the legislative process ran throughout 2023.

---

### ChatGPT Launch

**Date:** November 30, 2022

**Facts:** OpenAI released ChatGPT publicly on November 30, 2022, as a free research preview. The underlying model was GPT-3.5, a fine-tuned variant trained using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The system reached one million users within five days, according to Sam Altman. By January 2023 — two months after launch — it had reached 100 million monthly active users, a threshold that had taken Instagram approximately two and a half years and TikTok approximately nine months to reach. Swiss bank UBS described it as the fastest consumer internet ramp in twenty years of covering the sector. The interface was a conversational text box: no API required, no technical knowledge assumed.

**Interpretation:** ChatGPT did not introduce a new capability. GPT-3 had existed since 2020; capable language models were already running in API form for developers. What ChatGPT did was collapse the distance between the capability and the public. The interface was the breakthrough. By making the model conversational and accessible through a browser, OpenAI converted an impressive research artefact into a mass consumer experience. The 100 million figure is not just a growth statistic — it represents the moment AI stopped being something people read about and became something people used. Every subsequent conversation about AI regulation, economic disruption, and creative threat traces to this inflection point.

**Open questions:** The degree to which the RLHF fine-tuning rather than the interface change accounts for the quality improvement is still debated. Whether the chatbot format — optimised for helpfulness and fluency — created its own distortions (confident hallucination, sycophancy) is a live question that remains underexamined in coverage of this period.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** ChatGPT is the event that divided AI history into before and after for the general public; nearly every downstream consequence in this narrative traces to this launch.

**Sources:** "ChatGPT," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChatGPT; "Why ChatGPT Is the Fastest Growing Web Platform Ever," Time, 2023 — https://time.com/6253615/chatgpt-fastest-growing/

### Anthropic Founding and Constitutional AI

**Date:** April 2021 (founding); December 15, 2022 (Constitutional AI paper)

**Facts:** Anthropic was incorporated in April 2021, structured from its founding as a Public Benefit Corporation. Its co-founders were predominantly former OpenAI employees: Dario Amodei (CEO, previously VP of Research at OpenAI), Daniela Amodei (President, previously VP of Safety and Policy), along with Jared Kaplan, Jack Clark, Sam McCandlish, Benjamin Mann, Tom Brown, Christopher Olah, and others — eleven co-founders in total. Dario Amodei had departed OpenAI in December 2020; others followed. The stated cause of departure was disagreement over the pace and direction of commercialisation relative to safety work. On December 15, 2022, Anthropic published "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback" (arXiv:2212.08073), introducing a two-phase method for training safe AI assistants using a written list of principles (a "constitution") rather than relying on human labellers to identify harmful outputs. In the supervised phase, the model critiques and revises its own responses against the constitution; in the reinforcement learning phase, AI feedback (RLAIF) replaces human preference labels. Amazon committed $4 billion total (initial tranche: September 2023); Google committed $2 billion total (initial tranche: October 2023).

**Interpretation:** Anthropic represents the first institutionalised split in the AI industry over values rather than technology. Its founders did not leave OpenAI because they thought OpenAI was building the wrong thing — they left because they thought the right thing was being built too fast and with insufficient attention to what could go wrong. Constitutional AI matters beyond its technical contribution: it is Anthropic's answer to how you make safety work tractable at scale. Rather than assembling armies of human raters, you encode values in language and let the model reason against them. Whether this is adequate remains contested, but it is a coherent research programme, not a slogan. The Google and Amazon investments confirm that safety-first positioning is not commercially incompatible with serious capital.

**Open questions:** Whether Constitutional AI actually produces safer models, or merely models that appear safer on the metrics used to evaluate them, is genuinely unresolved. The tension between Anthropic's public benefit mission and the commercial pressures of competing with OpenAI is explicit — Dario Amodei has acknowledged it openly. Whether the founding values survive at scale is the defining long-term question for the organisation.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** Anthropic's founding establishes the institutional safety-versus-speed fault line that structures the whole subsequent debate; Constitutional AI is the first published technical methodology for encoding values into model behaviour at scale.

**Context notes:** Anthropic is an American AI safety company and public benefit corporation, founded by former OpenAI researchers who left over concerns that the pace of AI commercialisation was outrunning the development of adequate safety practices. Their approach — Constitutional AI — attempts to make AI systems harmless by training models to evaluate their own outputs against a written set of principles, rather than relying primarily on human labellers. This positions Anthropic in deliberate contrast to OpenAI: same foundational technology, different operating values. Dario Amodei (CEO) is a physicist by training who moved into AI research; Daniela Amodei (President) led operations and policy. Their sibling relationship and shared background gives Anthropic an unusual leadership coherence for a company founded on disagreement with a former employer.

**Sources:** "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback," Bai et al., Anthropic, December 2022 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073; "Anthropic," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic

### Sam Altman and the VC Ecosystem

**Date:** Ongoing character; key event: November 17–22, 2023 (OpenAI board crisis)

**Facts:** Sam Altman was born in 1985, enrolled at Stanford to study computer science, and dropped out after two years. In 2005, aged 19, he co-founded Loopt, a location-sharing app, as one of Y Combinator's early funded companies. In February 2014, he succeeded Paul Graham as President of Y Combinator. During his tenure, YC grew to fund approximately 1,900 companies, including Airbnb, DoorDash, Instacart, Reddit, and Twitch. He stepped down from YC in 2019 to become CEO of OpenAI. Y Combinator was founded on March 11, 2005, by Paul Graham, Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell, introducing the batch model of startup acceleration — cohorts accepted twice yearly for an intensive three-month programme culminating in Demo Day. YC's core philosophy, distilled from Paul Graham's essays, is "make something people want" — growth and user adoption treated as the primary signal of product validity. On November 17, 2023, OpenAI's board fired Altman, citing a failure to be "consistently candid" with the board. Within five days, after Microsoft moved to hire Altman, 702 of OpenAI's approximately 770 employees signed a letter threatening resignation unless the board resigned and Altman was reinstated. Altman returned as CEO on November 22, 2023, with a reconstituted board that replaced the directors who had fired him.

**Interpretation:** Altman is not primarily interesting as an individual; he is interesting as an archetype made consequential. Y Combinator established the dominant operating system for Silicon Valley: the startup as the fundamental unit of progress, growth as the primary metric of success, speed as a competitive virtue, and the founder as the irreplaceable asset. These values are load-bearing in how OpenAI behaves — not incidental. The November 2023 crisis crystallised the structural contradiction at the centre of OpenAI's existence. When the board — constituted to represent the non-profit mission — attempted to exercise oversight over the CEO, investors and employees overrode it within five days. The board lost. The lesson the industry absorbed was about where power actually sits when mission and momentum conflict. The VC ecosystem's financial stake in AI adoption is enormous: AI companies represent the largest concentration of venture capital in history, and returns depend on accelerating adoption.

**Open questions:** What the board members who fired Altman actually knew — the specific concerns that triggered the decision — has never been fully disclosed. The reconstituted board is less independent. Whether OpenAI's governance structure can meaningfully constrain its commercial trajectory is now an open and serious question.

**Historical weight:** significant
**Historical weight note:** The November 2023 crisis is the clearest demonstration of where power actually sits in AI governance, and it set the precedent that mission-oriented oversight boards are structurally weaker than investor and employee coalitions.

**Context notes:** Sam Altman represents a particular Silicon Valley tradition — the founder-operator whose legitimacy derives from the product's growth and whose authority derives from investor and employee confidence rather than formal governance structure. Y Combinator, which he led before OpenAI, is the most influential startup accelerator in history and the primary institutional transmitter of the "make something people want / move fast" value system. Understanding YC's philosophy is necessary context for understanding why OpenAI consistently prioritises product deployment over precautionary restraint: it is not recklessness, it is a deeply held and thoroughly elaborated theory of how progress happens. The VC ecosystem — the network of venture capital firms, limited partners, and portfolio companies that finances AI development — has an enormous financial interest in AI adoption accelerating. This is not a conspiracy; it is an alignment of incentives that shapes what gets built, what gets released, and at what pace.

**Sources:** "Sam Altman," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Altman; "Removal of Sam Altman from OpenAI," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removal_of_Sam_Altman_from_OpenAI

### GPT-4

**Date:** March 14, 2023

**Facts:** OpenAI announced GPT-4 on March 14, 2023 — the first OpenAI model to accept both text and image inputs, though outputs remained text only. On the Uniform Bar Exam, GPT-4 scored approximately in the top 10% of test takers; GPT-3.5 had scored approximately in the bottom 10% — a swing of roughly 80 percentile points on a professional credentialling exam between two successive model generations. On the LSAT, GPT-4 scored 163 (approximately 88th percentile) versus GPT-3.5's 149. On GRE Verbal reasoning, GPT-4 scored at the 99th percentile versus GPT-3.5's 63rd percentile. On the MMLU benchmark — 57 subjects, multiple choice — GPT-4 achieved state-of-the-art results in English and outperformed existing models in 24 of 26 languages tested. OpenAI stated the model achieved 75% on the Medical Knowledge Self-Assessment Program. OpenAI spent six months on iterative alignment before release, incorporating adversarial testing from over 50 external domain experts. The model was made available through ChatGPT Plus (paid tier) and the OpenAI API on the day of announcement.

**Interpretation:** GPT-4 is where the capability question became unavoidable for professions that had previously felt insulated. Moving from the bottom 10% to the top 10% on the bar exam in a single model generation is not an incremental improvement — it is a category change. Lawyers, doctors, and academics who had comfortably assumed professional-credentialling exams were beyond AI systems had to update those assumptions immediately. The multimodal input capability — reading images, diagrams, and documents — extended the relevant surface area beyond text-based tasks into visual and mixed-format work. GPT-4 marks the point at which "will AI affect knowledge work?" shifted from a speculative question to an empirical one with a clear provisional answer.

**Open questions:** The bar exam result has been contested — subsequent analysis suggests GPT-4's performance against practising test-takers who pass on first attempt was closer to the 40th–60th percentile depending on comparison cohort. OpenAI did not disclose the model's parameter count or architecture, sparking debate about the norms of scientific transparency in commercial AI research. The gap between benchmark performance and actual professional utility was underexplored at launch.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** GPT-4 made AI capability claims about professional work empirically concrete rather than speculative, and the bar exam result became the most-cited single data point in mainstream coverage of AI's economic implications.

**Sources:** "GPT-4," OpenAI, March 14, 2023 — https://openai.com/index/gpt-4-research/; "GPT-4 Technical Report," arXiv:2303.08774 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774

### EU AI Act

**Date:** March 13, 2024 (European Parliament vote); August 1, 2024 (entry into force)

**Facts:** The European Parliament adopted the EU Artificial Intelligence Act on March 13, 2024, by a vote of 523 in favour, 46 against, and 49 abstentions. Published in the EU Official Journal on July 12, 2024, and entering into force on August 1, 2024, it became Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 — the first comprehensive horizontal legal framework for AI regulation worldwide. The Act employs four risk tiers: unacceptable risk (prohibited), high risk (permitted with strict requirements), limited risk (transparency obligations), and minimal risk (no specific requirements). Prohibited systems — restrictions entering application February 2, 2025 — include government social scoring, AI exploiting psychological vulnerabilities, and real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement. High-risk systems — hiring, credit scoring, critical infrastructure, educational assessment, border control — face mandatory conformity assessments, human oversight requirements, and transparency documentation; obligations apply from August 2, 2026. Maximum penalties: €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover. Like GDPR, the Act applies extraterritorially to providers whose systems are used within the EU regardless of where the provider is based. Obligations for general-purpose AI models entered application August 2, 2025.

**Interpretation:** The EU AI Act is significant not because it immediately changes what AI systems can do, but because it establishes the compliance frame within which every company operating in Europe must plan. The EU has a consistent regulatory posture: it is rarely the innovation leader, but it moves first and most comprehensively on rules, and its market is large enough that companies comply rather than exit. GDPR became a de facto global standard not because other countries adopted it but because multinationals found it simpler to apply GDPR requirements universally. The AI Act is structured to produce the same dynamic. The risk-based tiering is a political compromise — it permits most commercial AI while drawing clear lines around applications most associated with authoritarian surveillance. Whether those lines are in the right places is a live debate; that they exist at all, and carry serious financial penalties, changes the incentive structure for the industry globally.

**Open questions:** The Act's treatment of general-purpose AI models, including LLMs, was contentious throughout negotiations and the final text is widely considered ambiguous. How national enforcement authorities will assess compliance for systems whose capabilities are difficult to characterise in advance is unresolved. Whether the risk-tier categories are durable — whether they capture what actually turns out to matter about AI risk — is an empirical question only answerable after deployment at scale.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The EU AI Act is the first binding comprehensive legal framework for AI anywhere in the world, and its extraterritorial reach means it functions as a de facto global compliance baseline for any company that operates in European markets.

**Context notes:** The European Union acts in this story as the third major pole alongside the United States and China — not as an innovator, but as the world's most active and consequential technology regulator. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), in force since 2018, established this pattern: a risk-based, rights-centred approach that became a global compliance standard simply because the EU market is too large to ignore. The AI Act follows this template deliberately. European regulatory philosophy differs from both the US approach (primarily market-driven, sector-specific rules) and the Chinese approach (state-directed development with selective restriction): the EU prioritises fundamental rights and risk mitigation over deployment speed, with fines scaled to global revenue designed to be felt by the largest companies in the world.
