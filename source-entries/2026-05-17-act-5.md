# Source Entries — Act 5: Infrastructure and Power (2023–2024)

Retrospective foundational entries. Three beats covering the physical and geopolitical layer beneath the AI model race.

### NVIDIA and the Compute Chokepoint

**Date:** May 30, 2023 (market cap milestone); H100 supply crisis ran throughout 2023

**Facts:** Jensen Huang co-founded NVIDIA in April 1993 with Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. NVIDIA's H100 GPU, built on the Hopper architecture using TSMC's N4 process with 80 billion transistors, launched in March 2023. The H100 features 80 GB of HBM3 memory and approximately 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth (SXM variant), with roughly nine times the training throughput of its predecessor on large language models. Retail pricing ranged from $25,000 to $40,000 per unit; secondary-market pricing during peak shortage reached $120,000 per unit. Lead times for H100 orders reached eleven months at peak; the shortage was compounded by a bottleneck in CoWoS advanced packaging, which integrates memory and compute die. On May 30, 2023, NVIDIA crossed a $1 trillion market capitalisation, becoming the seventh US company to reach that threshold. Revenue from data centre products surged as hyperscalers — Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta — competed for allocation. NVIDIA evaluated customers for H100 access based on model size, available infrastructure, and customer profile, effectively rationing supply. In 2023, NVIDIA held approximately 65% of the data centre AI accelerator market by revenue, with AMD at roughly 11%. US data centres consumed approximately 176 TWh of electricity in 2023, representing 4.4% of US annual electricity consumption; AI-optimised servers require two to four times the power per rack of traditional servers.

**Interpretation:** The H100 shortage of 2023 revealed something most observers had not yet processed: the AI race was constrained not by ideas or talent but by physical manufacturing capacity at two or three facilities on earth. NVIDIA did not merely build the best chip. Over fifteen years, starting with the 2006 release of CUDA — its parallel-computing platform and programming model — the company had built a software ecosystem that made switching to any alternative prohibitively expensive. Deep learning frameworks, optimised libraries, compilers, and two decades of researcher muscle memory were all written against CUDA. Hardware competitors could approach parity on raw specifications; they could not replicate the ecosystem. The result was a structural monopoly at the layer where AI performance is actually produced. The energy story ran alongside it: the requirement to cool and power dense H100 clusters was already straining grid infrastructure in Virginia, Iowa, and Oregon, turning electricity availability into a second physical constraint.

**Open questions:** Whether the CUDA moat is permanent is genuinely contested. OpenAI's Triton compiler and MLIR-based approaches suggest it may erode faster than NVIDIA expects. The longer-term question is whether custom silicon from Google (TPUs), Amazon (Trainium), or Microsoft will reduce hyperscaler dependence on NVIDIA, or whether network effects lock them in at the software layer regardless. The energy ceiling — how much grid infrastructure can actually be built in time — has not been stress-tested at full projected scale.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The H100 supply crisis established compute as the binding physical constraint of the AI era, a structural fact that has shaped every subsequent decision about who can build frontier models and who cannot.

**Context notes:** NVIDIA began as a graphics card company targeting video game rendering. GPUs perform many mathematical operations simultaneously in parallel, which happens to be exactly what training neural networks requires — matrix multiplications at scale. This made GPUs, rather than CPUs (which process operations sequentially), the substrate of the deep learning revolution. NVIDIA's CUDA, released in 2006, gave researchers a programming interface for this parallel compute — and because the AI research community standardised on CUDA early, virtually all deep learning software was written for NVIDIA hardware. A competitor offering faster or cheaper hardware still faces the cost of porting an entire software stack, which runs to months of engineering per project. This is the lock-in; the H100 is the latest and most extreme expression of it. Jensen Huang has been CEO since co-founding NVIDIA in 1993 — an unusual span that gives him deep authority over strategic decisions and a direct line to the company's founding technical vision.

### US Export Controls on AI Chips

**Date:** October 7, 2022 (initial rules); October 17, 2023 (expansion)

**Facts:** On October 7, 2022, the US Department of Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) published sweeping new export controls on advanced computing chips, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and supercomputing end-uses in China. The rules added high-performance GPUs — specifically the NVIDIA A100 and H100 — to the Commerce Control List, introduced new licensing requirements for chip exports above defined performance thresholds (600 GB/s aggregate interconnect bandwidth), and expanded the Foreign Direct Product Rule to cover advanced computing. NVIDIA responded by engineering export-compliant variants — the A800 (400 GB/s) and H800 (300 GB/s) — specifically to stay below the threshold while retaining substantial capability for Chinese customers. On October 17, 2023, BIS issued expanded rules that updated performance thresholds to close the A800/H800 loophole, extended controls to any processor built on architectures comparable to the H100, and added 28 Chinese firms to the Entity List. On the Chinese side, Huawei and SMIC demonstrated production of 7nm chips in 2023, showing domestic fabrication capacity previously assumed to be years away. Huawei's Ascend 910B AI chip, manufactured at SMIC, was designed to serve Chinese AI compute demand domestically.

**Interpretation:** The October 2022 rules marked the moment the United States government explicitly treated advanced AI compute as a strategic military asset and imposed controls accordingly — a significant doctrinal shift from decades of technology-export liberalisation. The October 2023 expansion reflected the speed of the policy game: NVIDIA had moved to compliant variants within months, demonstrating that performance-threshold rules invite engineering workarounds. BIS tightened the thresholds in response. The controls created a structural divergence: Chinese AI labs had significant pre-restriction inventory of H100s and A100s, but were cut off from future allocation as demand for compute accelerated. Huawei's Ascend response proved some domestic alternative capacity while simultaneously illustrating the gap: Ascend chips remained significantly behind NVIDIA's frontier hardware, and SMIC's yield and volume were constrained by the unavailability of ASML's EUV lithography equipment, itself subject to separate controls coordinated with the Netherlands.

**Open questions:** Whether the controls actually slowed Chinese AI capability development remains actively debated. Chinese labs demonstrated competitive frontier models (notably DeepSeek in late 2024) despite the restrictions, suggesting either sufficient stockpiled compute, effective use of constrained hardware, or algorithmic efficiency gains that partially compensate for hardware disadvantage. Whether allied states could independently enforce parallel controls, or whether Chinese customers would route orders through third countries, remained unresolved.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The October 2022 rules mark the point at which AI compute became an explicit instrument of great-power competition, ending the period in which technology competition was primarily commercial and beginning one in which state power shapes who can build frontier AI at all.

### Meta Releases Llama and the Open-Source AI Debate

**Date:** February 24, 2023 (Llama 1 announcement); March 3, 2023 (weights leaked); July 18, 2023 (Llama 2 commercial release)

**Facts:** On February 24, 2023, Meta AI announced LLaMA, models ranging from 7 billion to 65 billion parameters, released for research under an application process. On March 3, 2023, all LLaMA weights were uploaded and shared publicly via 4chan and spread through GitHub and Hugging Face. Within weeks, the research community had fine-tuned LLaMA into derivatives: Stanford released Alpaca on March 13, 2023 — a 7B model fine-tuned on 52,000 instruction-following examples at a total training cost under $600. On March 30, 2023, Vicuna-13B was released, rated by GPT-4 as evaluator at more than 90% of ChatGPT quality, trained at a cost of approximately $300. On July 18, 2023, Meta and Microsoft announced Llama 2 — models in 7B, 13B, and 70B sizes — free for research and most commercial use, with Microsoft Azure as preferred distribution partner. The commercial licence imposed restrictions on use cases violating Meta's acceptable use policy and a separate licence for applications serving more than 700 million monthly active users. Research published in 2023 showed that LoRA fine-tuning could remove Llama 2's safety training using a single GPU and under $200.

**Interpretation:** Meta's release of Llama — and especially the three weeks between the research-only release and the leak — compressed what would otherwise have been months of debate into a fait accompli. Once the weights were public, the derivative ecosystem was inevitable. The fine-tuning ecosystem demonstrated that frontier-quality instruction following could be reproduced for hundreds of dollars. This fundamentally changed the competitive landscape. OpenAI and Anthropic's safety investments — content filters, RLHF alignment — were trivially removable in the open-weight setting through cheap fine-tuning. Zuckerberg's public argument for open-source AI drew on the Linux analogy: openness builds trust, accelerates innovation, and prevents capture by a small number of companies. The counterargument — from safety researchers and US senators — was that open weights are categorically different from open-source software because the capability, once released, cannot be recalled or patched. Meta's position was also strategically self-serving in a way Zuckerberg acknowledged: as a company whose core product is social media rather than AI services, Meta has strong incentives to commoditise the model layer, reducing the competitive advantage of OpenAI and Google.

**Open questions:** Whether open-weights models are net positive or negative for safety remains genuinely unresolved. The empirical question — whether misuse facilitated by open models has caused documented harm at scale — is contested, partly because attribution is difficult. The definitional question is also unsettled: Llama's release of weights without training data or training code does not meet the Open Source Initiative's formal definition of open-source software, a distinction that matters for regulation and for the claims made on its behalf.

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** Llama's release and the fine-tuning ecosystem it catalysed established a permanent bifurcation between open-weight and closed-model AI development, with implications for safety, competition, and geopolitics that compound through every subsequent year.

**Context notes:** Meta (formerly Facebook) is a social media and advertising company, not primarily an AI product company. Its AI research division, FAIR, has long published foundational work, but Meta does not sell access to AI models as a core business. This gives Meta structurally different incentives from OpenAI or Anthropic: releasing powerful models freely does not cannibalise a revenue stream; it potentially disrupts competitors who do have one. "Open weights" means the trained numerical parameters of the model are made publicly downloadable — but it does not include the training data or the code used to train the model originally. This is a meaningful distinction from the full open-source software tradition, where all components needed to reproduce and modify the artifact are shared.
