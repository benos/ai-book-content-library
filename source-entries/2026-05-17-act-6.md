# Source Entries — Act 6: The Application Layer (2024–2025)

Retrospective foundational entries. Three beats covering the agentic turn, the AI design tool category, and the employment evidence base.

---

### Claude Computer Use: The Agentic Turn

**Date:** October 22, 2024

**Facts:** Anthropic released "computer use" as a public beta on October 22, 2024, integrated into an upgraded Claude 3.5 Sonnet model. The capability allows developers to direct Claude to interact with desktop interfaces — moving a cursor, clicking buttons, scrolling, and typing — by processing screenshots and returning pixel-coordinate instructions. Made available simultaneously via the Anthropic API, Amazon Bedrock, and Google Cloud Vertex AI. On the OSWorld benchmark — a standardised test of computer-control tasks — Claude scored 14.9% (screenshot-only mode) and 22.0% with extended step allocation. On SWE-bench Verified, the accompanying Claude 3.5 Sonnet scored 49.0%, up from 33.4% on the prior version. Early commercial partners included Replit, Asana, Canva, Cognition, DoorDash, and The Browser Company. Anthropic acknowledged the capability was "still experimental — at times cumbersome and error-prone," with particular difficulties in tasks involving scrolling, dragging, and zooming. OpenAI announced its comparable product, Operator, in November 2024 and released it in January 2025.

**Interpretation:** Computer use marks the clearest line yet between two fundamentally different things AI can be. Before this shift, a language model was a sophisticated answering machine: it received a query and returned text. Agentic AI does something categorically different — it takes sequences of actions in the world across multiple steps, using real interfaces, with consequences that persist beyond a conversation window. That changes the design problem entirely. A chat interface only needs to be legible; an agent needs to be trustworthy over time, auditable when it errs, and bounded in scope. The OSWorld scores — under 15% on the most constrained test — suggest the technology was nowhere near replacing human computer operation at release. But that undersells the significance: Anthropic was establishing the category and inviting developers to build toward it. OpenAI following within weeks confirmed this was a genuine competitive front, not a research curiosity. The agentic turn is also a safety inflection: an AI that types into forms and clicks purchase buttons is operating in the world in a way that a text generator is not.

**Open questions:** How quickly will benchmark performance translate to real-world task completion rates? What liability frameworks apply when an agent makes a consequential error — submitting the wrong form, purchasing the wrong item — on a user's behalf? Can context-window limitations be overcome to handle genuinely long-horizon tasks? Will agentic AI require new interface paradigms designed for machine interaction, or will it adapt to interfaces built for humans?

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The first frontier-model computer-use API released publicly established the agentic paradigm as a commercial product category and triggered an immediate competitive response from OpenAI — the point at which AI visibly shifted from conversation tool to acting agent.

**Context notes:** Agentic AI refers to AI systems that execute multi-step sequences of actions — navigating a browser, filling a form, running code, reading the result, and adjusting — in order to accomplish a goal, rather than merely responding to a single query. The distinction matters because action-taking creates persistent consequences in the world (a sent email, a submitted payment) that a text response does not. The term "agent" borrows from reinforcement learning, where agents are systems that perceive an environment and take actions to maximise a reward; applied to large language models, it describes a mode of operation rather than a separate architecture.

### The AI App-Builder Category: v0, Bolt, and Lovable

**Date:** October 2023 (v0 beta) — December 2024

**Facts:** Three products established the prompt-to-app category in rapid succession. Vercel launched v0 in beta in October 2023 — users type a plain-English description and receive production-ready React components styled with Tailwind CSS and shadcn/ui. Over 100,000 people joined the waitlist in the first three weeks; by 2024, more than 4 million users had generated over 100 million messages. StackBlitz launched Bolt.new on October 4, 2024 — announced via a single tweet — as a browser-based full-stack app builder using Claude 3.5 Sonnet as its underlying model, requiring no local development environment. Bolt reached $4 million in annualised recurring revenue within its first 30 days, $20 million ARR within 60 days, and $40 million ARR within five months. StackBlitz raised $105.5 million in a Series B in January 2025. Lovable launched under that name in November 2024 (previously GPT Engineer App, itself derived from the open-source gpt-engineer project with 50,000+ GitHub stars). By 2026, Lovable reported 6 million developers and 80,000 active teams, with an estimated $42 million ARR. The term "vibe coding" — describing the practice of using natural-language prompts to generate software rather than writing code directly — was coined by Andrej Karpathy on February 2, 2025, and named Collins English Dictionary Word of the Year for 2025.

**Interpretation:** These three products made the same bet at roughly the same moment: that the bottleneck to software creation was not ideas but implementation, and that a large enough model could bridge the gap. Their growth figures reveal the composition of the market — Bolt and v0 grew fastest among non-engineers, people who had never shipped code and now could at least at the prototype level. This is the AI application layer behaving as previous platform shifts have: the tool finds its users. The category also exposed a genuine tension. The tools were celebrated for enabling non-developers but criticised by professional developers for producing code that is functional but unmaintainable — hard to debug, tightly coupled, opaque to the person who nominally owns it. For the design and product community specifically, these tools arrived as a direct challenge to the prototyping phase: replacing Figma-to-developer handoff with prompt-to-deployed-app.

**Open questions:** What proportion of vibe-coded applications survive long enough to require maintenance — and what happens when they do? Does the category converge on a small number of survivors, as cloud infrastructure did, or remain fragmented? Will frontier labs absorb the category with their own tools, or will specialists retain advantage through tighter framework and deployment integration? Does lowering the barrier to software creation expand the software economy or redistribute existing demand?

**Historical weight:** significant
**Historical weight note:** The AI app-builder category produced the fastest consumer-software revenue growth in modern tech history (Bolt), validated a market that frontier labs subsequently entered, and gave a common name — vibe coding — to a new mode of software creation.

**Context notes:** Vercel is a US cloud platform specialising in hosting and deployment for JavaScript-based web applications, particularly those built on Next.js (a framework Vercel maintains). StackBlitz pioneered running Node.js development environments entirely inside the browser using WebContainers technology. The gpt-engineer open-source project was a community effort to build an AI coding agent before commercial equivalents existed; its GitHub traction signalled developer appetite for the category before Lovable had a product to sell.

### The Employment Picture: First Serious Data

**Date:** March 27, 2023 (Goldman Sachs report); January 14, 2024 (IMF report)

**Facts:** On March 27, 2023, Goldman Sachs economists Joseph Briggs and Devesh Kodnani published "The Potentially Large Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Economic Growth." Drawing on O*NET classifications of over 900 US occupations and the European ESCO database, the report estimated that 18% of work tasks globally could be automated by generative AI, affecting approximately 300 million full-time equivalent jobs. In the US and Europe, two-thirds of jobs were found to have some degree of AI exposure. Most exposed occupational categories: legal (approximately 40% of tasks substitutable) and office/administrative work (approximately 35%). The report also projected that widespread AI adoption could raise global GDP by up to 7% over a decade. On January 14, 2024, the IMF published "Gen-AI: Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work," estimating that 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, rising to approximately 60% in advanced economies. Of exposed jobs in advanced economies, roughly half were expected to benefit from AI augmentation while the other half faced genuine displacement risk. The IMF concluded AI is likely to worsen income inequality in most scenarios. At the firm level, an NBER working paper by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond — "Generative AI at Work," April 2023 — measured outcomes from deploying an AI customer-support assistant across 5,179 agents at a Fortune 500 company: productivity rose 14% on average, but gains were 34% for workers in the lowest-skill quintile and negligible for the highest-skilled workers. Subsequent research using ADP payroll data through 2025 found that workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations (software development, customer support) experienced employment declines of approximately 16% relative to pre-ChatGPT trend, while senior workers in the same fields remained stable. On Upwork, between November 2022 and February 2024: writing contracts fell 33%, translation fell 19%, customer-service contracts fell 16%.

**Interpretation:** The Goldman and IMF figures circulated at very different levels of abstraction, and this matters for how to read them. Both describe exposure — work technically addressable by AI — not replacement. The IMF's own modelling underscored this: roughly half of exposure translates to augmentation rather than displacement. These macro estimates are useful for establishing the ceiling of the effect, but they are poor guides to timing, which depends on adoption rates, regulatory friction, and implementation costs that vary enormously by sector. The firm-level evidence is more actionable and more troubling in specific ways. Brynjolfsson's customer-support study shows AI's distributional logic clearly: the gains accrue most to the least experienced workers and to the firms employing them, while the productivity differential that justified higher wages for experienced workers narrows. The Upwork freelance data captures a market that was among the first to feel displacement because it is frictionless — clients can stop commissioning writing work almost immediately once AI can substitute for it. The picture that emerges is not mass unemployment but concentrated early pressure on entry-level, task-definable, and output-measurable roles.

**Open questions:** How long is the lag between technological capability and measurable macro employment effects, and how much of current aggregate stability reflects that lag rather than a genuine plateau? Will productivity gains from AI augmentation translate into wage growth for workers who survive the adjustment, or accrue primarily to capital? How different will employment effects be between countries with strong employment protections (which slow displacement) and those without? Are the freelance market declines leading indicators for salaried employment, or is the freelance market structurally different in ways that limit generalisation?

**Historical weight:** landmark
**Historical weight note:** The Goldman Sachs and IMF reports were the first credible institutional analyses to put specific numbers on AI's labour-market exposure and immediately entered public and policy debate; alongside the first firm-level productivity studies, they defined the empirical vocabulary through which the employment question has been argued ever since.
